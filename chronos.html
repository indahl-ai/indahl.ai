Calendar Round

 

Of 18,980 days, about 52 years

The Long Count did not end in 2012. On 21 December 2012 it reached 13.0.0.0.0, the completion of the 13th b’ak’tun, a cycle of 144,000 days, about 394 years. The count rolled straight on into the next b’ak’tun, the same way an odometer rolls past a round number. The next b’ak’tun, 14.0.0.0.0, falls on 26 March 2407.

How it is built. The Long Count is a running count of days in five places: a k’in is one day, a winal 20 days, a tun 360 days, a k’atun 7,200 days and a b’ak’tun 144,000 days. Its epoch, written 13.0.0.0.0 4 Ajaw 8 Kumk’u, is 11 August 3114 BC in the proleptic Gregorian calendar. Beside it run two cycles. The Tzolk’in pairs a number from 1 to 13 with 20 day names, for 260 days. The Haab’ has 18 months of 20 days, numbered 0 to 19, and a closing period of 5 days, Wayeb’, for 365 days with no leap day, so it drifts against the seasons. A Tzolk’in and Haab’ pair returns every 18,980 days, the Calendar Round.

How it is kept. By counting days, with no observation. Turning ancient dates into Gregorian ones needs a correlation constant. This page uses the GMT correlation, Julian day 584283, the standard one. Others exist, such as 584285, which shift every date by two days. Maya communities in highland Guatemala and southern Mexico keep the 260-day count today.

How each calendar is kept

Gregorian

Counts from
AD 1, also written CE, an epoch set in the 6th century.
Year and month
Solar. 12 months, 365 days. A leap day, 29 February, comes every fourth year, except in century years not divisible by 400, for an average year of 365.2425 days.
Kept by
Fixed rule, introduced in 1582 to replace the Julian calendar.
Civil use
The civil calendar of most of the world.

Buddhist Era

Counts from
An epoch 543 years before AD 1, so the year is the Gregorian year plus 543.
Year and month
The Gregorian months and leap years. Since 1941 the year has begun on 1 January.
Kept by
Fixed rule, as Thailand’s official calendar.
Civil use
Dates on Thai documents and tickets. Neighboring countries count the Buddhist Era from other epochs or new years.

Islamic, Umm al-Qura

Counts from
AD 622, the year of the Hijra.
Year and month
Lunar. 12 months of 29 or 30 days, 354 or 355 days a year, with no leap month, so each year starts about 11 days earlier against the Gregorian calendar.
Kept by
A computed table. Each month is set from the predicted moon at Mecca, published in advance for Saudi Arabia.
Civil use
The official calendar of Saudi Arabia. Many other places begin each month on a sighting of the new crescent instead, so local dates can differ by a day.

Islamic, civil

Counts from
AD 622, the year of the Hijra.
Year and month
Lunar. Months alternate 30 and 29 days. In a 30-year cycle, 11 years add a day to the last month.
Kept by
Pure arithmetic, with no observation.
Civil use
Mainly for computing and for converting historical dates.

Hebrew

Counts from
An epoch in 3761 BC, written AM.
Year and month
Lunisolar. 12 months, or 13 in 7 years of every 19, when a 30-day month is added. Postponement rules give years of 353 to 355 or 383 to 385 days.
Kept by
A fixed arithmetic rule based on the mean new moon. The day begins at sunset.
Civil use
An official calendar of Israel, alongside the Gregorian.

Persian

Counts from
AD 622, the year of the Hijra, counted in solar years.
Year and month
Solar. The year begins at the March equinox, reckoned at the meridian of Tehran. 6 months of 31 days, 5 of 30, and a last month of 29 or 30.
Kept by
Astronomical computation of the equinox.
Civil use
The official calendar of Iran and Afghanistan.

Chinese

Counts from
No running count. Years are named in a 60-year cycle of stems and branches, such as bing-wu.
Year and month
Lunisolar. Each month begins on the day of the new moon in Beijing time. A leap month is added when a month holds no principal solar term, giving 12 or 13 months.
Kept by
Astronomical computation, published each year in China.
Civil use
Traditional dates in China and Chinese communities. China’s civil calendar is the Gregorian.

Korean

Counts from
The same 60-year cycle of year names. A traditional era count starts in 2333 BC.
Year and month
Lunisolar, with the same rules as the Chinese calendar, worked out for Korea’s meridian.
Kept by
Astronomical computation, published each year in Korea.
Civil use
Traditional dates in Korea. The civil calendar is the Gregorian.

Indian national

Counts from
AD 78, the Saka era.
Year and month
Solar. The year begins on 22 March, or 21 March in leap years. Chaitra has 30 days, or 31 in leap years; the next 5 months have 31 and the last 6 have 30. Leap years match the Gregorian.
Kept by
Fixed rule, adopted in 1957.
Civil use
India’s official civil calendar, alongside the Gregorian.

Ethiopian

Counts from
An epoch in AD 8, so the year runs 7 or 8 behind the Gregorian.
Year and month
Solar. 12 months of 30 days and a 13th month of 5 days, or 6 every fourth year without exception. The year begins on 11 September, or 12 September before a Gregorian leap year.
Kept by
Fixed rule.
Civil use
The official calendar of Ethiopia.

Coptic

Counts from
AD 284, the Era of the Martyrs.
Year and month
Solar, built like the Ethiopian: 12 months of 30 days and 5 or 6 more days at the end of the year.
Kept by
Fixed rule.
Civil use
Traditional and agricultural dates in Egypt.

Japanese

Counts from
The start of the current era. Reiwa 1 began on 1 May 2019. A new era begins with a new emperor.
Year and month
The Gregorian months and leap years, used since 1873.
Kept by
Fixed rule, with eras set by the government.
Civil use
Official documents in Japan, alongside the Gregorian year.

Republic of China

Counts from
1912, year 1 of the Republic of China.
Year and month
The Gregorian months and leap years.
Kept by
Fixed rule.
Civil use
Official dates in Taiwan.

Moon, table and rule

Two Islamic calendars sit side by side. The Umm al-Qura calendar follows the official table of Saudi Arabia, computed from the predicted new moon. The civil calendar is a fixed arithmetic cycle of 30 years. Many communities begin each month on a sighting of the new crescent, which no table can predict, so a local Islamic date can differ from both by a day.

The Chinese and Korean calendars use the same astronomical rules at two meridians, Beijing and Seoul, so a month can begin a day apart. This page works out each new moon itself, to within a minute, from the lunar theory in Jean Meeus’s Astronomical Algorithms, and dates it in Beijing time for the Chinese calendar and Korean time for the Korean. In 2027 the new moon falls a few minutes before midnight in Beijing and just after midnight in Seoul, so the Chinese new year is 6 February and the Korean 7 February.

The Hebrew and Islamic day begins at sunset, so their new year begins on the evening before the date shown. Countdowns here run to local midnight.

All dates come from the calendar data built into this browser. A browser without data for a calendar says so on its card.
